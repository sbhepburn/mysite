Old Wisdom

Thirty-eight named observations about how work, people, and organizations behave.

Each is named for whoever first recorded it: a naval historian, a friar, a pediatrician, a cyberneticist, a physicist who published his findings as a novel. Where the original wording survives, it is quoted directly, with a note on what has been trimmed and what is a later restatement.

None of them is enforceable, and several contradict one another. Each entry gives the claim, an example, a diagram of its shape, and a short note on applying it. No specialist knowledge is assumed anywhere in the collection.

I

Leverage

Entries 01 to 04

No. 01

Conway's Law

Melvin Conway, 1968, in a paper titled How Do Committees Invent? The observation has since been reproduced in studies of software architecture and product design.

…a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure.

Conway's own words, 1968, second half of the sentence.

A design tends to mirror the communication structure of the group that produced it. Where two teams coordinate closely, the parts they build fit together. Where they rarely speak, the parts meet at a seam. A bank with separate mortgage and savings departments generally produces separate applications, separate logins, and a customer who has to explain themselves twice.

In practice

Changing team boundaries changes the design. Where a seam in the product keeps causing problems, examine whether the two groups on either side of it have any reason to talk.

No. 02

Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety

W. Ross Ashby, An Introduction to Cybernetics, 1956. Ashby was describing regulators, biological and mechanical. Stafford Beer later applied the law to organizations.

Only variety can destroy variety.

Ashby's own words, 1956, in full. Variety means number of possible states.

A controller can absorb only as much variation as it can itself produce. If a situation presents forty distinct states and the controller has six distinct responses, thirty-four states go unmanaged, and responding faster does not help, because the shortfall is in range rather than speed. A help desk with four scripted answers cannot resolve a caller whose problem is of the fifth kind.

In practice

Count the kinds of problem arriving rather than the number. Where kinds exceed available responses, the remedy is to add responses: rules others can apply, delegated authority, or a system that absorbs the routine cases.

No. 03

Grove's Law of Managerial Output

Andrew Grove, High Output Management, 1983, written while he was president of Intel.

A manager's output = the output of his organization + … the neighboring organizations under his influence.

Grove's own formula, 1983, with one repeated phrase cut.

Grove defines a manager's output as the combined output of the groups they affect rather than the work they complete personally. An hour spent removing an obstacle for twelve people can produce more than an hour of the manager's own work, however good. The formulation also explains a common difficulty: a strong individual contributor promoted into management loses the measure of progress they are used to.

In practice

Before beginning a task, consider how many people the result reaches. Work that many people reuse, such as a decision, a template, or an explanation, generally returns more than work that reaches one.

No. 04

Task-Relevant Maturity

Grove, same book. The term describes how closely a manager should supervise a particular piece of work.

How closely you supervise depends on experience with this exact task, not on seniority.

Modern phrasing. Grove named the idea but never reduced it to a line.

Maturity here is measured per task rather than per person. The same experienced employee may need detailed direction on an unfamiliar assignment on Monday and none at all on a routine one on Tuesday. Supervision calibrated to seniority rather than to the specific task will be wrong in both directions, and the second error is the more expensive.

In practice

Set the level of involvement per task and say what it is: which work you expect to see in draft, and which you expect to hear about only when it is finished.

II

Triage

Entries 05 to 12

No. 05

The Theory of Constraints

Eliyahu Goldratt, The Goal, 1984, a management text written as a novel about a manufacturing plant.

An hour lost at a bottleneck is an hour lost for the entire system.

Goldratt's own words, The Goal, 1984.

In any system, one step limits total throughput. Improving a step upstream of that constraint adds work waiting in front of it. Improving a step downstream adds idle capacity. Neither changes the quantity finished. A clinic with one scanner does not see more patients by adding a second receptionist.

In practice

Find where work accumulates and waits, and improve that step first. Relieving a constraint moves it elsewhere, so the analysis has to be repeated once the change takes effect.

No. 06

Little's Law

John Little, 1954, who proved that the relationship holds for any stable queue.

How long something takes equals how much is in progress divided by how fast you finish things.

Modern phrasing. Little's 1954 original is the formula L = λW.

The time an item spends in a system equals the quantity of work in progress divided by the rate of completion. Doubling the number of items underway doubles the wait for each, while the quantity finished stays the same. A team running nine projects at once completes them no faster than a team running three; the other six are waiting.

In practice

Limit the number of items in progress. Only two variables appear in the relationship, and since completion rate is difficult to change quickly, the practical lever is starting fewer things.

No. 07

Amdahl's Law

Gene Amdahl, 1967, arguing about the limits of parallel computing. The arithmetic applies to any process composed of parts.

How much faster the whole thing can get is capped by the share of it you are improving.

Modern phrasing. Amdahl's 1967 sentence is about parallel computing and unreadable outside it.

The maximum improvement to a whole process is bounded by the share of total time the improved part accounts for. If a step represents five percent of the time, eliminating it entirely saves five percent. This is why efficiency programmes often disappoint: effort goes to the step that is easiest to measure or most familiar, rather than the one consuming the most time.

In practice

Measure the distribution of time before choosing what to improve. The ceiling on any improvement is fixed in advance and can be calculated before the work begins.

No. 08

One-Way and Two-Way Doors

Jeff Bezos, in Amazon shareholder letters, describing how large organizations become slow.

Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible…

Bezos's own words, shareholder letter, 2015. He calls those doors one-way.

Bezos separates decisions by reversibility. Most are reversible and can be made quickly by whoever is closest to them. A few are not, and warrant deliberation and consultation. Applying equal care to both categories is what produces a slow organization, since the great majority of decisions do not require it.

In practice

Ask what reversing this decision would cost in six months. Where reversal is inexpensive, decide now. Where it is expensive, gather more information and more perspectives.

No. 09

The Pareto Principle

Vilfredo Pareto observed in 1896 that a small share of the population held most of Italy's land. Joseph Juran generalized the pattern to quality management and named it after him.

Roughly 80 percent of the effects come from 20 percent of the causes.

Modern phrasing. Pareto wrote about land; Juran named it the vital few and the trivial many.

In many distributions, a small number of causes account for most of the effect. The specific ratio varies and the two figures need not sum to one hundred; what holds is the shape. A small number of customers, defects, or products usually carries most of the weight, and the remainder forms a long tail.

In practice

Identify the concentrated portion before optimizing anything. The inverse also applies: the final increment of refinement often costs the largest share of the effort, which is worth paying in some places and not in others.

No. 10

Brooks's Law

Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month, 1975, drawn from his experience managing the OS/360 project at IBM.

Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.

Brooks's own words, 1975, in full. It generalises past software.

Adding people to a project already behind schedule tends to delay it further. New arrivals must be brought up to speed by the people currently holding the deadline, and each addition increases the number of communication paths: three people maintain three, eight maintain twenty-eight. The added capacity arrives later than the added coordination cost.

In practice

Where a project is late, reducing scope is usually more effective than adding people. If capacity must be added, assign a separable piece of work with a boundary of its own.

No. 11

Parkinson's Law

Cyril Northcote Parkinson, The Economist, 1955. He had observed that Admiralty administrative staff grew while the fleet shrank.

Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion.

Parkinson's own words, 1955, in full.

A task tends to occupy whatever time is allotted to it. Given three weeks, a status report acquires appendices. Given ten minutes, it becomes three sentences. The additional time is generally consumed by scope, revision, and formatting rather than by the quality of the underlying work.

In practice

Fix the deadline before deciding the contents. A shorter allocation forces an explicit decision about what the task actually requires.

No. 12

Hofstadter's Law

Douglas Hofstadter, Godel, Escher, Bach, 1979, on the difficulty of estimating complex work.

It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law.

Hofstadter's own words, 1979, in full.

Estimates run long even after they have been padded, because the padded estimate comes from the same source as the original and omits the same unknowns. Complex work conceals its own complexity: the individual surprises are not visible until the work is underway, by which point a date has usually been given.

In practice

Use recorded durations of comparable past work rather than intuition. Historical figures already contain the surprises that fresh estimates leave out.

III

Preservation

Entries 13 to 19

No. 13

Chesterton's Fence

G. K. Chesterton, The Thing, 1929. He describes two reformers who find a fence across a road. The second declines to remove it until the first can explain its purpose.

If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away.

Chesterton's own words, 1929. The speaker is the wiser of two reformers.

The principle concerns the order of operations rather than preservation for its own sake. Many long-standing constraints exist because of an incident nobody now remembers. A form field that appears redundant may have been added after a regulatory finding. From the outside, a constraint doing its work is indistinguishable from one doing none.

In practice

Establish the original reason before removing the constraint. Where it is genuinely obsolete, remove it. Where it is not, record the reason so that the next person does not have to repeat the inquiry.

No. 14

Gall's Law

John Gall, Systemantics, 1975. Gall was a pediatrician who wrote about the ways systems fail.

A complex system that works … evolved from a simple system that worked.

Gall's own words, 1975, with four words cut from the middle.

Complex systems that work are generally found to have grown from simple systems that worked. Complexity is discovered in operation rather than anticipated in design, so a large system specified in full before use tends to meet conditions its designers did not model. Most durable institutions and products began as something much smaller that functioned.

In practice

Deliver the smallest version that works for real users, then extend it. A plan with no functioning state until late in its schedule has no mechanism for learning from operation.

No. 15

Hyrum's Law

Hyrum Wright, a software engineer, describing what happens to any interface with a large number of users.

…all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody.

Wright's own words. The full version opens: with a sufficient number of users.

With enough users, every observable behavior of a system becomes a dependency, including behaviors that were never promised and some that were considered defects. Changing the order of rows in a report, the wording of a confirmation, or a form's tolerance for a blank field will break somebody's established routine.

In practice

Before changing anything with many users, assume that incidental behaviors are in use. Announce changes to those as well as to documented features.

No. 16

The Second-System Effect

Brooks again, 1975. He described the second system a designer builds as the most dangerous one.

The second is the most dangerous system a man ever designs.

Brooks's own words, 1975, in full.

Replacements tend to accumulate every feature the first version omitted, together with the stored complaints of everyone who used it. The result is larger, later, and sometimes worse at the original system's principal function. The energy behind such a project is real, but a list of grievances is not a specification.

In practice

Write down the small number of things the replacement must do better, and treat each addition to that list as a schedule decision rather than a scope decision.

No. 17

Kernighan's Law

Brian Kernighan and P. J. Plauger, The Elements of Programming Style, 1974.

if you're as clever as you can be … how will you ever debug it?

Kernighan's own words, 1974, trimmed. He wrote it about programs; it travels.

Diagnosing a fault in something is harder than building it, so anything built at the limit of a person's ability cannot reliably be repaired by that same person. Circumstance compounds the difficulty: repairs happen later, often under time pressure, and often by someone who does not hold the original reasoning.

In practice

Prefer the version an unfamiliar colleague could follow. Where explaining a mechanism takes longer than rebuilding it plainly, that difference is a reasonable estimate of its maintenance cost.

No. 18

The Law of Leaky Abstractions

Joel Spolsky, 2002, on the limits of simplified interfaces.

All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky.

Spolsky's own words, 2002, in full.

Every simplification of a complicated system eventually exposes the complexity beneath it. A steering wheel conceals an engine; a one-page summary conceals a department. Both work almost always, which is the reason they exist. When they fail, only someone who understands the underlying layer can respond. Abstractions reduce the effort required, not the knowledge required.

In practice

Use the simplified view for routine work, and maintain access to someone who understands what it conceals.

No. 19

The Lindy Effect

Named after Lindy's, a delicatessen in New York where the idea was first discussed in relation to comedians' careers. Described by Albert Goldman in 1964 and later formalized by Benoit Mandelbrot and Nassim Taleb.

For a thing that does not age, every year survived predicts another year of survival.

Modern phrasing. Goldman's 1964 original is about comedians and television slots.

For things that do not age, such as ideas, technologies, and texts, expected remaining life increases with age. A book in print for four hundred years is likelier to remain in print than one published last year. Living things behave in the opposite way. A process that has run for twelve years has absorbed twelve years of unusual conditions, which is evidence not otherwise obtainable.

In practice

When comparing an established option with a new one, treat the years the established one has already survived as evidence of robustness, and replace it for a specific reason rather than for its age.

IV

Reading the Organization

Entries 20 to 27

No. 20

Naur's Theory Building

Peter Naur, 1985, in an essay arguing that what a team produces is a shared understanding rather than a document.

…the programmers form or achieve … a theory, of the matters at hand.

Naur's own words, 1985, trimmed. Read programmers as anyone who builds a thing.

The people who build something hold a model of why it works as it does: what was attempted, what failed, which parts are fragile, and what a particular exception protects. Much of this is never written down, because it is not consciously held until someone asks. When those people leave, the artifact remains and the model does not.

In practice

Treat the understanding as the asset being preserved. Recording reasons is more useful than recording procedures, since procedures can be inferred from the artifact and reasons cannot.

No. 21

The Bus Factor

An informal measure of concentration risk, of unknown authorship, in common use in project management.

How many people would have to be hit by a bus before the work becomes unmaintainable?

Modern phrasing. No known author, and the bus is the whole reason it is memorable.

The bus factor is the number of people who would have to become unavailable before work could not continue. For many important processes the answer is one: a single person knows the exception, holds the supplier relationship, or remembers why a figure is calculated a particular way. The exposure is invisible until it materializes.

In practice

Establish the bus factor for each critical process and record it. Where the answer is one, the correction is shared work over time; documentation alone rarely transfers enough.

No. 22

The Shirky Principle

Attributed to Clay Shirky, describing the incentives that act on established institutions.

Institutions will try to preserve the problem to which they are the solution.

Shirky's own words, in full.

An organization built to address a problem acquires an interest in that problem continuing, since budget, headcount, and purpose depend on it. This rarely takes the form of deliberate obstruction. It appears as a durable sense that the problem is more complicated than it looks and that proposed simplifications carry drawbacks.

In practice

Where a simplification meets unusually detailed resistance, examine what depends on the current arrangement, and consider what the group's role would be afterwards.

No. 23

Goodhart's Law

Charles Goodhart, 1975, on monetary targets. The phrasing in general circulation is Marilyn Strathern's, from 1997.

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Strathern's own words, 1997, sharpening Goodhart's longer 1975 original.

A measure is a proxy for something that matters, and the relationship between them holds only while nothing depends on it. Once the measure carries consequences, effort moves toward the measure and the proxy relationship weakens. Calls answered rises; problems solved does not. This generally occurs without anyone acting in bad faith.

In practice

Pair each target with a second measure that would detect the obvious ways of satisfying the first, and state plainly what the target is a proxy for.

No. 24

Parkinson's Law of Triviality

Parkinson, 1957. A committee in his example has three items on its agenda: a reactor, a bicycle shed, and the coffee budget.

The time spent on any item … in inverse proportion to the sum involved.

Parkinson's own words, 1957, trimmed. The sum means money, not importance.

Discussion time runs inversely to the sum at stake. The ten million pound reactor was approved in two and a half minutes. The three hundred and fifty pound bicycle shed took forty-five. The coffee budget, at twenty-one pounds a year, took an hour and a quarter and was deferred. Everyone can assess a bicycle shed; few will admit to not following a reactor.

In practice

Compare time spent per item against the sums involved. An item that passes without discussion may have been well understood, or may not have been examined at all.

No. 25

Segal's Law

An adage of uncertain origin, in circulation since the 1930s.

A man with a watch knows what time it is. A man with two watches is never sure.

The canonical form of an anonymous 1930s joke. No original text survives.

Two sources of the same information that nearly agree produce less certainty than one source, because every decision now begins with reconciling them. The reconciliation is recurring rather than one-time: the discrepancy reappears with each new figure, and the argument is repeated by whoever is in the room.

In practice

Designate one source as authoritative and derive the others from it. The cost of occasional error from a single source is usually lower than the standing cost of reconciliation.

No. 26

Sturgeon's Law

Theodore Sturgeon, science fiction writer, responding to critics who judged the genre by its weakest examples.

Ninety percent of everything is crap.

Sturgeon's own words, in full. He called it a revelation, not a law.

Sturgeon's argument was comparative: a large majority of output in any field is unremarkable, so judging a field by its average says little about the field. Read as calibration, it sets an expectation. Weak proposals, unconvincing demonstrations, and poor first drafts are the normal distribution rather than evidence that something has gone wrong.

In practice

Evaluate a category by its best examples, and treat the filtering as a planned cost with hours attached rather than an unexpected one.

No. 27

The Dunning-Kruger Effect

David Dunning and Justin Kruger, Cornell, 1999, published under the title Unskilled and Unaware of It.

The less you know about something, the harder it is to see how much you do not know.

Modern phrasing. The 1999 paper is titled Unskilled and Unaware of It.

The finding is that assessing one's competence at a task draws on the same knowledge as performing it, so those with the least skill are also the least able to recognize the gap. Experts show the opposite pattern: familiarity with a field reveals its extent, which produces qualified statements and the appearance of uncertainty.

In practice

Ask a confident person what would change their mind, and ask a hedging expert for a single best estimate.

On the evidence

The widely circulated curve with a peak and a valley does not appear in the paper. Statisticians have since argued that part of the measured effect follows from the method of analysis. The weaker claim is the one that holds: confidence is a poor indicator of accuracy.

V

Judgment

Entries 28 to 38

No. 28

Occam's Razor

Attributed to William of Ockham, a fourteenth-century friar, who used the principle in theological argument. The familiar Latin phrasing is later than he is.

Plurality must never be posited without necessity.

Ockham's own words, in translation. The famous entities version is not his.

Where two explanations account for the same evidence, prefer the one requiring fewer independent assumptions. Plurality here means the number of separate things that must be true. An explanation with six moving parts requires all six to hold. An unanswered message is more often explained by a full inbox than by a decision about the sender.

In practice

List what each candidate explanation requires, then count. Begin with the shortest list. The principle indicates where to start rather than what is true.

No. 29

Hanlon's Razor

Attributed to Robert J. Hanlon, from a 1980 collection of aphorisms. Comparable formulations appear earlier in Goethe and others.

Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity.

The canonical form, 1980. Stupidity is the original word; it is harsher than the truth usually is.

Behavior that reads as hostile is more often produced by time pressure, fatigue, or missing context. Malice requires planning and sustained attention, both of which are scarcer than the alternatives. A curt reply usually indicates a short interval between reading and responding rather than a judgment about the reader.

In practice

Ask what a rushed and reasonably well-intentioned person would have produced in the same circumstances, and compare that with what arrived.

Caveat

The razor is an opening assumption rather than a settled one. Repeated errors in a single direction that consistently favour one party call for a different explanation. A further refinement is also worth holding: behavior read as incompetence is often a reasonable response to constraints and incentives not visible from outside.

No. 30

Inversion

Carl Jacobi, the nineteenth-century mathematician, on approaching difficult problems. Charlie Munger adopted it as a method for decisions.

Invert, always invert.

Jacobi's own words, in translation. Munger borrowed it and made it a habit.

Where the conditions for success are numerous and hard to specify, the conditions for failure are usually few and easy to state. Asking what would cause a project to fail produces a short concrete list within minutes: the sponsor's attention moves elsewhere, the underlying data proves wrong, ownership is undefined after launch.

In practice

At the start of a project, write the account of its failure a year from now, then treat the resulting list as the plan.

No. 31

Cynefin

Dave Snowden, 1999. The Welsh word refers to the several places a person belongs to at once.

Complicated problems have expert answers. Complex ones only yield to small experiments.

Modern phrasing. Snowden calls the two the domain of experts and the domain of emergence.

The framework separates problems whose answers are knowable from those whose answers are not. Complicated problems have been solved before and yield to expertise. Complex problems involve interacting parts whose behavior cannot be predicted in advance, and yield only to small trials observed closely. Applying either method to the other kind of problem is expensive.

In practice

Establish first whether this specific problem has been solved before. If it has, acquire the answer. If it has not, design a small trial and decide in advance what result would end it.

No. 32

The Map Is Not the Territory

Alfred Korzybski, 1931, in a paper on the relationship between language and the things it describes.

A map is not the territory it represents…

Korzybski's own words, 1931, trimmed. The rest adds that a good map shares the territory's structure.

A representation is necessarily simpler than what it represents, and is made at a particular moment for a particular purpose. An organization chart describes reporting lines rather than how decisions are actually made. A process diagram describes the intended path rather than the one used when it is blocked. Disagreement between the two is information about the representation.

In practice

Where representation and observation conflict, observe directly and then revise the representation. The discrepancy usually indicates something the representation had no place to record.

No. 33

The Law of the Instrument

Abraham Kaplan, 1964, and Abraham Maslow, 1966, who gave the idea its familiar form.

If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail.

Modern phrasing. Maslow's 1966 sentence was longer, and Kaplan got there first with a small boy.

The tools available shape which problems are recognized and which solutions are considered. A group fluent in one method will find that most requests suit that method, often correctly, since familiarity genuinely lowers cost and risk. The effect is that alternatives go unevaluated, because nobody present can represent them well enough to make the case.

In practice

Have the problem described by someone who works with different tools before selecting an approach. An unusually confident estimate for the familiar option is worth examining.

No. 34

Survivorship Bias

From Abraham Wald's 1943 analysis of damage to returning aircraft, conducted for the Statistical Research Group.

You only see the ones that made it back, and they are not a fair sample.

Modern phrasing. Wald's 1943 work is a statistical memo with no aphorism in it.

Wald was asked where to add armour, based on damage recorded on aircraft that returned. He observed that the sample excluded aircraft that did not return, so the undamaged areas on survivors were the likely fatal locations. The pattern recurs wherever a sample is drawn from outcomes: supplier references, published case studies, and systems still in operation.

In practice

Before drawing a conclusion from successful cases, look for cases that followed the same approach and failed. If none can be found, the sample is incomplete rather than uniform.

No. 35

Regression to the Mean

Francis Galton, 1886, from measurements of height across successive generations.

Extreme results are usually followed by less extreme ones, with or without your help.

Modern phrasing. Galton called it regression towards mediocrity.

Extreme measurements contain an element of chance, and chance does not persist, so a subsequent measurement tends to fall closer to the average. This produces a durable illusion in evaluation: praise appears to be followed by decline and criticism by improvement, because both are applied to outliers, and outliers move toward the middle regardless.

In practice

Establish a baseline before intervening, and compare results against the expected movement rather than against the extreme value that prompted the intervention.

No. 36

The Peter Principle

Laurence J. Peter, 1969. The book was written as satire and received as management theory.

In a hierarchy every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence.

Peter's own words, 1969, in full.

Promotion rewards performance in one role and assigns a different one. Where the two require different skills, a sequence of promotions ends at the first role the person does not perform well, and stops there, since further promotion is not offered. The mechanism requires no error of judgment at any single step.

In practice

Evaluate candidates against the requirements of the role being filled rather than performance in the previous one, and make lateral and downward moves ordinary rather than exceptional.

No. 37

Hick's Law

William Hick and Ray Hyman, working independently in the early 1950s, measuring reaction time against the number of possible signals.

The time it takes to choose grows with the number of options, but only gently.

Modern phrasing. The original is a formula: RT = a + b log₂(n + 1).

Decision time increases with the logarithm of the number of options, so doubling the options adds a fixed increment rather than doubling the time. The result holds for organized sets. Where options are unsorted and unranked, the observed behavior is different: people defer the decision rather than take proportionally longer over it.

In practice

Group and rank options rather than reducing their number arbitrarily, and supply a default so that choosing is optional rather than required.

No. 38

Illich's Law

Associated with Ivan Illich, whose published work applies the argument to institutions rather than to hours worked.

Beyond a certain threshold, human efficiency decreases, even becoming negative.

Widely attributed to Illich. His own books make the same argument about institutions.

Beyond a threshold, additional input reduces output. Illich's own version concerns institutions: past a certain scale, a system begins to obstruct the purpose it was built for, so that a transport network can consume more time than it saves. Applied to individual work, the eleventh hour introduces errors that consume the ninth and tenth.

In practice

Establish where the threshold falls for a given kind of work and treat it as a limit. Output past that point is negative rather than merely diminished.

A closing note

The value of a named pattern is that it can be recognised early, before the evidence is in.

Which is also the reason to hold each of them loosely.

Using them

Several of these contradict one another, and that is expected. Gall's Law argues for growing a system from a small working version; the Second-System Effect warns about what the small version left out. The Lindy Effect argues for leaving a long-running arrangement alone; Chesterton's Fence argues for establishing why it exists first. Determining which applies to a particular situation is the substance of the work, and no collection can do it for you.

Each entry is a compressed observation made in a particular setting, usually a factory, a laboratory, a committee, or a government office. The compression is what makes them portable, and it is also what makes them approximate at the edges. They are most useful as a first hypothesis, and least useful as a conclusion.
